Lecture 1 — Foundations: from 1800 to 1978

Slides (online)Slides (PDF)

Wednesday 21 October 2026 · Block: history and institutions · 3 hours

Questions

  • How did a great economy arrive at the revolution of 1949?
  • What is a planned economy, how did China build one, and what did it do?
  • What does the Great Leap Forward famine teach us about plans, prices and information?
  • What would it take to say that the later reforms caused China's growth?

Key concepts

Price and relative scarcity · quantities versus prices as ways to coordinate an economy · command economy · state-set prices · investment cycles ("leaps" and retrenchments) · the Material Product System versus GDP · hukou (introduced; it returns in lectures 4-5) · famine and institutions.

In class

  1. Welcome, what "using history, doing economics" means, the seven blocks of the course, and the rules of the journey.
  2. China on the map, and the way the West talks about "China".
  3. 1800 to 1949 in a handful of slides: the geography of the population, the economy around 1800, its strains, silver and opium, the Opium Wars, Japan, early industrialisation, war and civil war.
  4. 1949-1978: how a plan works; the pendulum of investment; the 1958 household registration system; the Great Leap Forward and the famine; the turn of 1978.
  5. A question for the next lectures: did the reforms cause the growth, or would it have happened anyway?

An anonymous opening poll is part of the class. It is not graded.

Readings

Required

  • Barry Naughton, The Chinese Economy: Transitions and Growth (MIT Press, 2007): chapter 2, "The Chinese Economy Before 1949" (pp. 33-54), and chapter 3, sections 3.1-3.3, "The Socialist Era, 1949-1978" (from p. 55).

Optional

  • Friedrich A. Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society", American Economic Review 35(4), 1945, pp. 519-530 — on what prices do.
  • Xin Meng, Nancy Qian and Pierre Yared, "The Institutional Causes of China's Great Famine, 1959-1961", Review of Economic Studies 82(4), 2015, pp. 1568-1611 — read the introduction.

Think about (ungraded)

In 1959 China's grain output per person fell by about 15% relative to 1958 but stayed above subsistence, yet millions died.

  1. In an ordinary shortage, where would you expect more deaths: where output is lower or where it is higher?
  2. In 1959 the data show the opposite: regions with higher output per person had higher mortality. Which feature of the economic system could reverse the sign?
  3. There is no county-level output data for the famine. How would you measure how severe it was in each county?

Short written answers, one or two paragraphs per point, through the online form announced in class. No login, not graded.
