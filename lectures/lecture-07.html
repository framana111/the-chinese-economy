Lecture 7 — Reforming the state firms

Slides (online)Slides (PDF)

Wednesday 11 November 2026 · Block: reallocation within sectors

Questions

  • What wedges do you see in the economy where you grew up, and who gains from them?
  • What did China do with its state firms between 1998 and 2007, and what was it worth?
  • Can making bad firms better make the economy worse?
  • How can you tell which firm is good when you cannot measure its productivity?
  • What happens when a wedge disappears overnight: who grows, who leaves?

Key concepts

Wedges on capital, on size and on entry · MRPL and MRPK · TFPQ and TFPR · the same markup for every firm · "grasp the large, let go of the small" · closure and privatization · surplus workers · open and closed economy · a level, not a growth rate · export quotas and licences · the end of the quotas on 1 January 2005 · new exporters and exiters · prices as a measure of productivity · three ways to see misallocation.

Readings

On AulaWeb, enrolled students find the papers listed here, one zip file per lecture. Other books and a few older papers are not there, and some papers are there in their working-paper version.

Required

  • Chang-Tai Hsieh and Zheng (Michael) Song, "Grasp the Large, Let Go of the Small: The Transformation of the State Sector in China", Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, Spring 2015, pp. 295-366 — the introduction, the facts on state and private firms, and the counterfactuals (Table 8).
  • Amit K. Khandelwal, Peter K. Schott and Shang-Jin Wei, "Trade Liberalization and Embedded Institutional Reform: Evidence from Chinese Exporters", American Economic Review 103(6), 2013, pp. 2169-2195 — the introduction, the history of the quotas, and the results on who grew after 2005. On AulaWeb: the working-paper version (NBER Working Paper 17524, 2011).

Optional (works cited on the slides)

  • The comments by Loren Brandt and Amit Khandelwal on Hsieh and Song, in the same issue of the Brookings Papers.
  • Chang-Tai Hsieh and Peter J. Klenow (2009): see lecture 6.
  • Marc J. Melitz and Sašo Polanec (2015), the "dynamic Olley-Pakes" decomposition used in the SOE Lab's panel "Where productivity growth comes from".
