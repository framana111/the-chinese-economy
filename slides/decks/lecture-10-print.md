## From yeast to mushrooms
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>Up to 2007, China's productivity grew like <strong>yeast</strong>: in almost every industry, through opening, investment and copying foreign technology</li>
<li class="fragment">Even then, <strong>72%</strong> of manufacturing productivity growth came from firms <strong>entering and exiting</strong> (lecture 8). New firms replacing old ones: <strong>creative destruction</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">Growth from <strong>innovation</strong> looks like mushrooms: a few firms, a few products, hard to predict</li>
</ul>

<p class="fragment" data-fragment-index="3" style="font-size:.52em;margin:.9em 0 .2em">Today, in three steps:</p>
<ol class="fragment" data-fragment-index="3" style="font-size:.5em;line-height:1.35">
<li><strong>The facts</strong>: does China innovate? R&D, patents, and two warnings about quality</li>
<li><strong>Two models</strong>: the quality ladder; imitate or innovate. With the Ladder Lab</li>
<li><strong>One paper</strong>: is China's R&D real? Chen, Liu, Suárez Serrato and Xu (2021), in seven steps</li>
</ol>

## Many patents. How good?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul style="font-size:.52em;line-height:1.35">
<li>Of the 2024 patent applications by Chinese applicants, only <strong>6.9%</strong> were also filed abroad: the lowest share among the top countries. An invention not worth protecting abroad is usually worth less</li>
<li class="fragment">Utility models, the lighter kind of patent: almost all the world's are Chinese</li>
<li class="fragment">When China overtook the US as the largest patent filer (2011), the growth came mostly from firms that had <strong>never patented</strong> before, and patents became less linked to R&D and to productivity. Subsidies and targets likely played a part (Hu, Zhang and Zhao, 2017)</li>
<li class="fragment">The same question for R&D: when the state rewards <strong>reported</strong> R&D, how much of it is real? That is today's paper</li>
</ul>

## The quality ladder
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:22px">
<figure style="margin:0;flex:none;text-align:center;width:470px"><img src="assets/l10/ladder.svg" style="width:470px;display:block"><figcaption style="font-size:.38em;opacity:.7;line-height:1.25;margin-top:4px">An illustration, not data</figcaption></figure>
<ul style="font-size:.47em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:430px;flex:none">
<li>Every product can be made better. Growth is a <strong>sequence of better versions</strong>: rungs on a ladder</li>
<li class="fragment">Whoever does the research and succeeds climbs a rung. If it is a <strong>new firm</strong>, it takes the market from the old leader: <strong>creative destruction</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">The reward: a monopoly profit, until the next climber arrives. So growth depends on how many firms try, how often they succeed, and how big each step is</li>
<li class="fragment">The old leader loses. So old leaders have a reason to <strong>keep new firms out</strong>: licences, credit, local protection</li>
<li class="fragment">Aghion and Howitt (1992). Nobel Prize 2025, shared with Joel Mokyr, "for having explained innovation-driven economic growth"</li>
</ul>
</div>

## The model, in words
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p style="font-size:.4em;opacity:.7;margin:0 0 .5em">Acemoglu, Aghion and Zilibotti (2006), told the way a paper sets out its model</p>
<div style="display:grid;grid-template-columns:1fr 1fr;gap:14px 26px;width:880px;margin:0 auto">
<div style="text-align:left;font-size:.43em;line-height:1.32;border-top:3px solid #555;padding-top:6px"><div style="font-size:.74em;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:.08em;opacity:.8;margin-bottom:.25em">1 · THE ECONOMY</div>A country with productivity <strong>A</strong>. The best in the world, the <strong>frontier</strong>, grows 2% a year. What matters is <strong>a</strong>, the country's productivity as a share of the frontier: a = 0.3 means 30% of it</div>
<div class="fragment" data-fragment-index="1" style="text-align:left;font-size:.43em;line-height:1.32;border-top:3px solid #555;padding-top:6px"><div style="font-size:.74em;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:.08em;opacity:.8;margin-bottom:.25em">2 · TWO WAYS TO GROW</div><strong style="color:#8ab4f8">Copy</strong>: adopt technology that already exists at the frontier. The further behind you are, the more there is to copy. <strong style="color:#ffcf5a">Build</strong>: improve your own technology. It pays the same at any distance</div>
<div class="fragment" data-fragment-index="2" style="text-align:left;font-size:.43em;line-height:1.32;border-top:3px solid #555;padding-top:6px"><div style="font-size:.74em;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:.08em;opacity:.8;margin-bottom:.25em">3 · TWO STRATEGIES</div><strong style="color:#8ab4f8">Imitation</strong>: copy a lot, build little. It works with big incumbents, long relationships, lots of investment. <strong style="color:#ffcf5a">Innovation</strong>: copy less, build more. It needs <strong>selection</strong>: new firms, the best managers, the worst replaced</div>
<div class="fragment" data-fragment-index="3" style="text-align:left;font-size:.43em;line-height:1.32;border-top:3px solid #555;padding-top:6px"><div style="font-size:.74em;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:.08em;opacity:.8;margin-bottom:.25em">4 · THE RESULT</div>Far from the frontier, imitation grows faster; close to it, innovation does. The switch point is a threshold, <strong>a*</strong>. A country that never switches stops short of the frontier: <strong>the trap</strong></div>
</div>

<div style="width:410px;flex:none;text-align:left">
<p style="font-size:.44em;line-height:1.35;margin:0 0 .6em">In one line: productivity this year = <strong>η</strong> × the frontier last year (<span style="color:#8ab4f8">copy</span>) + <strong>γ</strong> × your own last year (<span style="color:#ffcf5a">build on it</span>)</p>
<ul style="font-size:.46em;line-height:1.3;margin:0">
<li><strong>a</strong> is small: far from the frontier, there is a lot to copy. <strong>Imitate and invest</strong> pays most. Big incumbents, long relationships, lots of investment, protection: they work. Yeast</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>a</strong> is close to 1: little left to copy. Growth needs <strong>innovation</strong>, and innovation needs <strong>selection</strong>: new firms, the best managers, the worst ones replaced. Mushrooms</li>
<li class="fragment">The two lines cross at <strong>a*</strong>: there the best strategy changes</li>
<li class="fragment">The danger: the institutions that are best for catching up become a <strong>trap</strong> if a country keeps them too long (Acemoglu, Aghion and Zilibotti, 2006)</li>
</ul>

## Notching R&D
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Chen, Liu, Suárez Serrato and Xu (2021) · 1. Motivation</p>
<p style="font-size:.44em;margin:0 0 .6em;opacity:.8">"Notching R&D Investment with Corporate Income Tax Cuts in China", American Economic Review, 2021</p>

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:22px">
<figure style="margin:0;flex:none;text-align:center;width:460px"><img src="assets/l10/notch.svg" style="width:460px;display:block"><figcaption style="font-size:.38em;opacity:.7;line-height:1.25;margin-top:4px">Tax rate on profits of a large firm (sales above 200 million yuan) against its R&D as % of sales, from 2008. <ul style="font-size:.47em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:440px;flex:none">
<li>Governments everywhere cut taxes for firms that do R&D. But "firms can also respond by relabeling other expenses as R&D"</li>
<li class="fragment">China's <strong>InnoCom</strong> programme: a firm certified "high-tech" pays <strong>15%</strong> tax on its profits, not 25% (33% before 2008)</li>
<li class="fragment">The condition: R&D of at least <strong>3%</strong> of sales for large firms, <strong>4%</strong> for medium, <strong>6%</strong> for small (5% for all before 2008). Also: 60% of sales from high-tech products, 30% of workers with a degree</li>
<li class="fragment">Cross the line and the low rate applies to <strong>all</strong> profits: a notch. A big reward for a small step</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Question
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Chen et al. (2021) · 2. Question</p>
<p class="qtext">When the state cuts the tax of firms that report enough R&D, do they do more research, or do they record other costs as "R&D"? And what does real R&D do to productivity?</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li class="fragment"><strong>How much more R&D?</strong> The response at the line</li>

<p class="qkick">Chen et al. (2021) · 3. Data</p>

<li>Corporate tax returns from China's tax administration (the SAT, the Chinese IRS), 2008-2011: about <strong>1.2 million</strong> observations, some <strong>300,000</strong> manufacturing firms a year. For 2006-2007: the statistics bureau's factory survey</li>

## Robustness
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Chen et al. (2021) · 6. Robustness, or what a referee would ask</p>

<ul style="font-size:.5em;line-height:1.35">
<li><strong>Do firms cut their reported sales instead?</strong> No: sales are smooth across the line. Nor do firms inflate sales to reach a bigger size class, with a lower line</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Does the drop in costs appear at any line?</strong> At another size group's line: no drop</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Is the drop a real reorganisation?</strong> Even if a quarter of it were real, the estimates barely change</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Special firms?</strong> Without state-owned, low-tech or low-profit firms: the same response</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>What it cannot show alone</strong>: the 24.2% and the 9% come from a model and its assumptions. The drop in costs is a pattern, not proof of a cause; the authors say so</li>
</ul>

## Why it matters
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Chen et al. (2021) · 7. Why it matters</p>

<ul style="font-size:.48em;line-height:1.33">
<li>Part of China's R&D boom is <strong>on paper</strong>: about a quarter of the R&D reported by the firms that jump the line. For the whole economy the paper gives no number</li>
<li class="fragment">The real part pays as in rich countries. The low return of Chinese R&D found by other studies is partly an effect of relabelling</li>
<li class="fragment">Design: a bigger tax cut, or a lower line, pulls in firms with worse research opportunities, and more relabelling. A line with registration and audits lets the state check few firms. In the model, with a quarter relabelled, it costs less per unit of real R&D than a standard tax credit</li>
<li class="fragment">Worth the lost tax? Only if one firm's R&D also helps others (<strong>spillovers</strong>). It breaks even if a doubling of all firms' R&D raises each firm's productivity by <strong>6.9%</strong>. Other studies find about 20%</li>
<li class="fragment"><a href="labs/ladder-lab.html" target="_blank" style="color:#8ab4f8">Ladder Lab</a>, panel B: R&D subsidy +100%, 24% relabelled. Reported R&D doubles, real R&D rises less, growth less still</li>
</ul>
