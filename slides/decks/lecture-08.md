## Perspiration or inspiration?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul style="font-size:.6em">
<li>Output per worker can grow for two reasons: <strong>more inputs</strong> per worker (capital, schooling), or <strong>more output from the same inputs</strong> (productivity, TFP)
<ul style="font-size:.9em"><li>Y = A K<sup>γ</sup> L<sup>1−γ</sup>, so Y/L = A (K/L)<sup>γ</sup>. With the same workers, add <strong>K</strong> or raise <strong>A</strong>: Y/L rises either way</li></ul></li>
<li class="fragment">The sceptics: East Asia grew by <strong>perspiration</strong>, not inspiration. Like the Soviet Union, it would slow down when inputs stop growing</li>
<li class="fragment">For China, Alwyn Young (2003) corrects the official data and cuts productivity growth outside agriculture, 1978-1998, from <strong>3%</strong> a year to <strong>1.4%</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">"Gold into base metals": was China's productivity miracle <strong>creative accounting</strong>?</li>
</ul>

## What China promised
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li><strong>Tariffs</strong>: average applied tariff from 15.6% (2001) to 9.7% (2005), and bound</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>The right to trade</strong>: within three years, every firm in China may import and export directly, without a state licence
<ul style="font-size:.9em"><li>Except a list of goods kept for state trading companies (grain, cotton, crude oil, tobacco and a few more). Done in 2004: a registration replaces the approval</li></ul></li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Safeguards</strong> for the other WTO members: they may cap a surge of Chinese imports. On textiles until 2008, on any product for 12 years</li>
<li class="fragment">A <strong>"non-market economy"</strong> clause for anti-dumping cases, for 15 years
<ul style="font-size:.9em"><li><strong>Dumping</strong>: selling abroad below the price at home, or below cost. The importing country may then add an <strong>anti-dumping duty</strong></li>
<li>"Non-market economy": Chinese prices and costs may be ignored and replaced by a third country's. Dumping becomes easier to find, and the duties higher</li></ul></li>
<li class="fragment">The same rules everywhere in China, zones included</li>
</ul>

## Few firms export
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>China, 2004: the <strong>small</strong> firms are 80% of all industrial firms, but make 10% of output and <strong>2.5%</strong> of exports</li>

<li class="fragment">In the US, only <strong>18%</strong> of manufacturing firms export. Everywhere we have firm data, exporters are a <strong>minority</strong>, <strong>bigger</strong> and <strong>more productive</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">A model with identical firms cannot explain this</li>

## Selection, or learning-by-exporting?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul style="font-size:.52em">
<li>Exporters are better. Because they <strong>were</strong> better (selection), or because exporting <strong>made</strong> them better?</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Learning-by-exporting</strong>:
<ul style="font-size:.9em"><li>foreign buyers demand higher quality</li><li>international standards are tougher than domestic ones</li><li>meeting other markets, and other cultures, brings new ideas</li></ul></li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Hard to identify</strong>:
<ul style="font-size:.9em"><li>the ideal experiment: send some firms abroad <strong>at random</strong>, and compare them with the others</li>
<li>the threat: the best firms <strong>choose</strong> to export, so comparing exporters with the others mixes the two (lecture 3)</li>
<li>De Loecker (2007), Slovenia: firms become more productive <strong>after</strong> they start exporting, and the gap keeps widening; more so when they sell to rich countries. With a model-based (structural) method, not an experiment</li></ul></li>
</ul>

## Motivation
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Brandt et al. (2017) · 1. Motivation</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>Chinese manufacturing got much more productive in 1998-2007, mostly through entry and exit (Brandt, Van Biesebroeck and Zhang, 2012)</li>
<li class="fragment">Most studies look at <strong>exports</strong>. Few look at what happened when China let foreign goods <strong>in</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">Zhu Rongji wanted the WTO as a <strong>catalyst</strong> for reforms he could not impose at home</li>
<li class="fragment">Did foreign competition make Chinese firms <strong>better</strong>, or only take away their <strong>margins</strong>?</li>
</ul>

## Results: markups
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Brandt et al. (2017) · 5. Results</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li><strong>Markup</strong> = price / marginal cost. The median Chinese firm: about <strong>1.26</strong>, a price 26% above marginal cost</li>
<li class="fragment">A lower tariff on what the sector <strong>sells</strong> (foreign rivals come in): markups <strong>down</strong>
<ul style="font-size:.9em"><li>each point of tariff cut: markup about 0.1% lower (1.26 → 1.259)</li><li>all the cuts of 1998-2007 together: about 1% lower (1.26 → about 1.25). Small</li></ul></li>
<li class="fragment">A lower tariff on what the sector <strong>buys</strong> (its inputs): costs fall, markups <strong>up</strong>. Firms keep part of the saving
<ul style="font-size:.9em"><li>the same import can be both: cheaper steel is a <strong>rival</strong> for Chinese steelmakers, a cheaper <strong>input</strong> for carmakers</li></ul></li>
</ul>

<p class="fragment" style="font-size:.48em;margin-top:.8em;opacity:.85">Careful: in Melitz markups never move. Falling markups need another model (Melitz and Ottaviano, 2008)</p>

## Robustness
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Brandt et al. (2017) · 6. Robustness, or what a referee would ask</p>

<ul style="font-size:.52em">
<li><strong>The authors found their own mistake.</strong> In 2019 they corrected a programming error in a price index. Markups: unchanged. Firms already there: the productivity gain is no longer clear</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Only tariffs?</strong> In the same years: the right to trade for all firms, sectors opened to foreign investment, the state firms of lecture 7
<ul style="font-size:.9em"><li>How they separate them: the cuts followed a rule (the WTO maximum), unrelated to the state firms' share; and they compare only sectors within the same broad industry, in the same year</li>
<li>What they find: the tariff effects survive. State firms react less: they grow and close less than private firms when protection falls</li></ul></li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>A black box</strong>: productivity and markups come from estimated production functions</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Who is missing</strong>: small firms, and everything outside manufacturing</li>
</ul>
