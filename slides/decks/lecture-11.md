## Externalities
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p style="font-size:.58em;line-height:1.3;border-left:5px solid #ffcf5a;padding-left:.6em;text-align:left;margin:0 0 .7em">An <strong>externality</strong>: a cost or a benefit that my choice puts on <strong>someone else</strong>, who did not choose it, and that <strong>nobody pays for</strong></p>

<ul style="font-size:.5em;line-height:1.33">
<li class="fragment">A cost: a factory's smoke falls on the city around it. Lecture 5: more pollution, more <strong>skilled workers leaving</strong> the city</li>
<li class="fragment">A benefit: you get vaccinated, and you also protect the people you meet</li>
<li class="fragment">Each of us compares <strong>our own</strong> costs with <strong>our own</strong> gains. So we get <span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">too much of what hurts others, too little of what helps them</span></li>
<li class="fragment">The fixes: a <strong>tax or a subsidy</strong> that puts the missing cost or gain back on whoever decides (InnoCom's tax cut); a <strong>property right</strong> (a patent); a <strong>rule</strong> (a limit on smoke)</li>
<li class="fragment">Lecture 9: Harberger expected growth like yeast, everywhere at once, from "broad and general externalities, such as growth in knowledge"</li>
</ul>

## Ideas leak, rivals lose
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:grid;grid-template-columns:1fr 1fr;gap:14px 30px;width:900px;margin:0 auto">
<div style="text-align:left;font-size:.44em;line-height:1.34;border-top:4px solid #8fc59a;padding-top:6px"><div style="font-size:.74em;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:.08em;color:#8fc59a;margin-bottom:.3em">+ · IDEAS LEAK</div><div>A loaf of bread feeds one person. An idea, a design, once made "can be used as often as desired, in as many productive activities as desired": it is <strong>non-rival</strong></div><div class="fragment" data-fragment-index="1" style="margin-top:.4em">And it is hard to keep: rivals take your product apart, hire your engineers, read your patent. Your idea helps them, and they <strong>do not pay you</strong>: a <strong>knowledge spillover</strong>. So firms do <strong>too little</strong> R&D</div><div class="fragment" data-fragment-index="2" style="margin-top:.4em">A <strong>patent</strong> (lecture 10) lets you stop copies for some years, in exchange for publishing the idea: a partial fix</div></div>
<div class="fragment" data-fragment-index="3" style="text-align:left;font-size:.44em;line-height:1.34;border-top:4px solid #ef8a6f;padding-top:6px"><div style="font-size:.74em;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:.08em;color:#ef8a6f;margin-bottom:.3em">− · RIVALS LOSE</div><div>The quality ladder (lecture 10): your better product takes the market of the <strong>old leader</strong></div><div style="margin-top:.4em">You count your new profit, not the profit the old leader loses: <strong>business stealing</strong>. So firms can do <strong>too much</strong> R&D: part of the prize is a transfer, not something new</div></div>
</div>

<p class="fragment" data-fragment-index="4" style="font-size:.48em;line-height:1.32;margin:.8em auto 0;width:900px;text-align:left">Aghion and Howitt (1992): two forces pull opposite ways, so growth "may be more or less than socially optimal". Which one wins is a question for data. <span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">Studies of R&D usually find that spillovers win</span>: other studies find 20%, Chen et al. needed 6.9%</p>

## The model, in words
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">König et al. (2022) · 4. Empirical strategy: a model</p>

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:22px">
<figure style="margin:0;flex:none;text-align:center;width:500px"><img src="assets/l11/kssz-model.svg" style="width:500px;display:block"><figcaption style="font-size:.36em;opacity:.7;line-height:1.25;margin-top:4px">Firms (dots) on the rungs of productivity. An illustration, not data</figcaption></figure>
<ul style="font-size:.44em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:420px;flex:none">
<li>Every year each firm chooses. <strong style="color:#8ab4f8">Imitate</strong>: free. Meet a firm at random; if it is better, you may copy one step. <strong style="color:#ffcf5a">Innovate</strong>: pay for R&D and try a step on your own</li>
<li class="fragment">Who innovates? Firms near the top: few firms are better than them, so copying rarely works. Lecture 10's rule, now <strong>firm by firm</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">A firm hit by a wedge keeps less of any gain, so it does less R&D; a favoured firm does more</li>
<li class="fragment"><span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">The externality: a firm that climbs becomes someone to copy</span>. The firms below that meet it learn from it, and nobody pays it</li>
<li class="fragment">How they estimate it: a black box. They pick the model's numbers so that it reproduces the curves of the data</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Results: most growth is copying
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">König et al. (2022) · 5. Results · 6. Robustness</p>

<ul style="font-size:.5em;line-height:1.33">
<li>The model fits: <strong>12%</strong> of firms do R&D (data: 15%); TFP grows <strong>3.6%</strong> a year (data: 3%). Growth was not used to choose the numbers</li>
<li class="fragment">Where does growth come from, in a year? <strong>20%</strong> from innovation, <strong>80%</strong> from imitation</li>
<li class="fragment">But stop innovation for good, and growth in the long run is <strong>zero</strong>. <span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">Copying needs someone to copy from</span></li>
<li class="fragment">Taiwan, same model: "both innovation and technology diffusion are faster in Taiwan". With Taiwan's technology of R&D, China would grow <strong>faster</strong>, even with its own wedges</li>
<li class="fragment">Robustness: a stricter definition of R&D firm; another way to measure productivity; Taiwan's data. Same patterns</li>
</ul>

<li class="fragment">"Too much of a good thing": <span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">if everybody innovates, nobody is left copying</span>, and copying was 80% of growth</li>

## Why it matters
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">König et al. (2022) · 7. Why it matters</p>

<ul style="font-size:.48em;line-height:1.33">
<li>Not how much R&D, but <strong>who</strong> does it: "it is important to induce the 'right firms' to pursue an innovation strategy"</li>
<li class="fragment">Fewer wedges: a <strong>double dividend</strong>. A better allocation today, and subsidies that reach the right firms</li>
</ul>
<p class="fragment" data-fragment-index="2" style="font-size:.46em;margin:.6em 0 .2em;text-align:left;width:900px;margin-left:auto;margin-right:auto">The externalities in this model:</p>
<ul class="fragment" data-fragment-index="2" style="font-size:.45em;line-height:1.32">
<li><strong style="color:#8fc59a">+ Climbing teaches</strong>: the firms below copy whoever climbed, by R&D or by copying. The main one</li>
<li><strong style="color:#ef8a6f">− +</strong> <strong>Through the market</strong>: a rival that improves takes your customers (business stealing); a growing economy buys more from everyone (demand)</li>
<li><strong style="color:#8fc59a">+ From abroad</strong>, in an extension: copying the world frontier. With half the wedges, China ends at <strong>81%</strong> of the frontier's productivity, not 46%</li>
</ul>
<p class="fragment" style="font-size:.44em;margin:.5em 0 0">Left out: what the subsidies cost, and whether firms do too much or too little R&D overall</p>

<li class="fragment">A rough calculation: without the demand subsidies, China would have had about <strong>277,000 more deaths</strong> from PM2.5 in 2004-2020, 5.1% more than the deaths attributed to it</li>
<li class="fragment">Carbon (CO2): the same direction, smaller</li>
<li class="fragment"><span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">The subsidy that does least for the industry does most for the air</span></li>
<li class="fragment">Two externalities: smog falls on the <strong>city</strong>, carbon on the <strong>planet</strong>. Who has a reason to pay for each?</li>
</ul>

## Do the neighbours win or lose?

## Why it matters
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Banares-Sanchez et al. (2026) · 7. Why it matters</p>

<ul style="font-size:.47em;line-height:1.32">
<li><strong>Climate</strong> is a global externality: the usual answer is treaties, which are hard to agree. Here <strong>one country's</strong> industrial policy made solar cheaper <strong>for everyone</strong>. That is the "ray of hope"</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Industrial policy</strong> can pay, here: benefits above costs for Chinese citizens even without counting the climate</li>
<li class="fragment">The best target is the <strong>market failure</strong>: knowledge spillovers, hence innovation subsidies. But design matters: Chen et al. showed how firms relabel</li>

<li class="fragment"><strong>Limits</strong>: only Chinese citizens are counted. Cheaper panels lower China's own export prices; producers abroad lose business, and the EU and the US answered with tariffs. The world-wide balance is the authors' next paper</li>
