## The story of the model
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="font-size:.58em;line-height:1.45;text-align:left;max-width:920px;margin:0 auto">
<p style="margin:.2em 0">Firm $i$ produces with capital and labour: &nbsp; $Y_i = A_i\,K_i^{\alpha}L_i^{1-\alpha}$, &nbsp; $0&lt;\alpha&lt;1$</p>
<p class="fragment" style="margin:.4em 0">Each firm sells its own product; to sell more it must lower its price $P_i$</p>
<p class="fragment" style="margin:.4em 0">It pays $(1+\tau_{L,i})\,w$ per worker and $(1+\tau_{K,i})\,r$ per unit of capital. Cheap credit: $\tau_K&lt;0$. Pressure to keep workers: $\tau_L&lt;0$</p>
<p class="fragment" style="margin:.4em 0">It hires until the last worker and the last unit of capital pay for themselves:</p>
<p class="fragment" style="text-align:center;margin:.3em 0">$MRPL_i \propto \dfrac{P_iY_i}{L_i} = (1+\tau_{L,i})\,w \qquad MRPK_i \propto \dfrac{P_iY_i}{K_i} = (1+\tau_{K,i})\,r$</p>
<p class="fragment" style="margin:.4em 0">So we can <strong>see</strong> them in the data: <strong>revenue per worker</strong> and <strong>revenue per unit of capital</strong>, firm by firm. Without wedges they are the same everywhere</p>
</div>

---

## Two TFPs
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="font-size:.58em;line-height:1.45;text-align:left;max-width:920px;margin:0 auto">
<p style="margin:.2em 0"><strong>TFPQ</strong>: output in <strong>physical units</strong> per unit of input &nbsp; $TFPQ_i = A_i = \dfrac{Y_i}{K_i^{\alpha}L_i^{1-\alpha}}$</p>
<p style="margin:.4em 0"><strong>TFPR</strong>: output in <strong>yuan</strong> per unit of input &nbsp; $TFPR_i = P_iA_i = \dfrac{P_iY_i}{K_i^{\alpha}L_i^{1-\alpha}}$</p>
<div class="fragment" style="margin:.6em 0;padding:.4em .7em;border-left:3px solid #ffcf5a">
Two steel mills. Mill B makes <strong>twice</strong> the steel of mill A with the same inputs. Without wedges, B gets more capital and workers and sells more steel; its price falls. It stops growing when one more yuan of inputs earns <strong>the same</strong> as at mill A
</div>
<div class="fragment"><p style="margin:.4em 0">So without wedges <strong>TFPR is the same</strong> in every firm. With wedges:</p>
<p style="text-align:center;margin:.2em 0">$TFPR_i \propto (1+\tau_{K,i})^{\alpha}\,(1+\tau_{L,i})^{1-\alpha}$</p></div>
<p class="fragment" style="margin:.4em 0">Differences in <strong>TFPQ</strong> are technology and management: a wedge changes how much a firm uses and sells, not what it makes with each unit. Differences in <strong>TFPR</strong> are the <strong>wedges</strong></p>
</div>

---

## Rules of the world
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul>
<li>Every firm faces the <strong>same</strong> wage and interest rate; only the wedges differ</li>
<li class="fragment">All firms of an industry use the <strong>same</strong> mix: the same $\alpha$, taken from the same industry in the United States</li>
<li class="fragment">All firms charge the <strong>same</strong> markup. Demand, with $\sigma = 3$:<br>$Y_i = Y\,(P_i/P)^{-\sigma}$, so price $= 1.5\times$ marginal cost<br><span style="font-size:.8em;opacity:.85">$P$, $Y$: price and output of the industry; $1.5 = \sigma/(\sigma-1)$</span></li>
<li class="fragment">So <strong>everything</strong> that makes revenue per input differ across firms is counted as a <strong>wedge</strong>, including errors in the data</li>
</ul>

## Question
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Klenow (2009) · 3. Data</p>
<p class="qtext">What if the data are wrong?</p>

<ul>
<li class="fragment">An error in a firm's revenue or capital looks <strong>exactly like a wedge</strong>: the model counts it as misallocation</li>
<li class="fragment">So the measured gains are <strong>too large</strong> if the data are noisy, and noisier data in China and India would explain part of the gap with the US</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>SOE Lab</strong>: open "Errors in the data". The same error for all firms, then a bigger one for state firms. What happens to the loss we <strong>measure</strong>? And to the true one?</li>
<li class="fragment">Their check: pure noise would not depend on <strong>who owns the firm</strong>. It does: state plants have about <strong>40% lower</strong> TFPR</li>
</ul>

## A level, not a growth rate
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul>
<li>"+30-50%" is a <strong>level</strong>: how much higher TFP would be, in a given year, with a better allocation</li>
<li class="fragment">The model is <strong>static</strong>: the wedges are frictions, reallocation happens once. There is nothing in it that changes the growth <strong>rate</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">Growth appears only when the gap <strong>changes</strong>: allocation in China improved by about <strong>15%</strong> from 1998 to 2005, about <strong>2% a year</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">TFP in Chinese industry grew about 6% a year: so "perhaps <strong>one third</strong>" of it came from better allocation, while the gap was closing</li>
</ul>

## Robustness
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Klenow (2009) · 6. Robustness</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li><strong>Demand</strong>: with $\sigma = 5$ instead of 3, the gains get <strong>bigger</strong>: China in 2005 from +87% to +184%</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Outliers</strong>: they cut the 1% tails of the distributions before computing anything</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Technology</strong>: let each plant have its own capital share instead of a capital wedge; the gaps remain</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Noise</strong>: TFPR is linked to ownership, as policy would predict and noise would not</li>
</ul>

## Why it matters
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Klenow (2009) · 7. Why it matters</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>Part of what makes a country poor is <strong>where</strong> its capital and workers are: good firms too small, bad firms too big</li>
<li class="fragment">In China a visible wedge is <strong>state ownership</strong>: cheap credit, soft budgets, workers kept on the payroll</li>
<li class="fragment">Between 1998 and 2005 the gap narrowed, as the state firms were closed, sold and reformed. <strong>How</strong>, and what was it worth? Tomorrow</li>
</ul>

## Additional material: the formula behind the gains
<!-- .slide: class="dense" id="hk-formula" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Klenow (2009), equations (2.15)-(2.16)</p>

<div style="font-size:.6em;line-height:1.5;text-align:left;max-width:900px;margin:0 auto">
<p style="margin:.2em 0">Industry TFP, given the allocation:</p>
<p style="text-align:center;margin:.3em 0">$TFP_s = \left[\sum_{i}\left(A_i\,\dfrac{\overline{TFPR}_s}{TFPR_i}\right)^{\sigma-1}\right]^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}$</p>
<p class="fragment" style="margin:.4em 0">If TFPQ and TFPR are jointly log-normal:</p>
<p class="fragment" style="text-align:center;margin:.2em 0;font-size:.82em">$\begin{aligned}\log TFP_s = {} & \dfrac{1}{\sigma-1}\log\left(\textstyle\sum_i A_i^{\sigma-1}\right)\\ & - \dfrac{\sigma}{2}\,\mathrm{var}\left(\log TFPR_i\right) - \dfrac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\,\mathrm{var}\left(\log \dfrac{MRPK_i}{MRPL_i}\right)\end{aligned}$</p>
<ul class="fragment" style="font-size:.85em;margin:.4em 0 0">
<li>Three terms: how good the firms are; how badly inputs are spread among firms; firms with the wrong mix of capital and workers</li>
<li>With $\sigma = 3$, the second term is 1.5 times the variance of log TFPR</li>
<li>Equation (2.16) of the working paper leaves out the third term, which is zero only if all firms have the same ratio of capital wedge to labour wedge</li>
</ul>
</div>
