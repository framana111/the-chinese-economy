## Hsieh and Klenow in one picture
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul>
<li>All firms have the <strong>same technology</strong>; they differ in how good they are (TFPQ)</li>
<li class="fragment">Without wedges, capital and workers move until each one earns the <strong>same</strong> everywhere: same MRPK, same MRPL, so the <strong>same TFPR</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">A wedge keeps a firm <strong>too big or too small</strong>: its TFPR moves off the line</li>
<li class="fragment">The loss: the output we could have with the <strong>same</strong> capital and workers</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>SOE Lab</strong>: preset 1998, look at the bars, press "Reallocate". Then open "Production" under the lab</li>
</ul>

## Two TFPs, in two firms
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Lecture 6, once more, with numbers</p>

<div style="font-size:.5em">
<table style="margin:0 auto;border-collapse:collapse">
<thead><tr><th></th><th>TFPQ</th><th>Capital and workers</th><th>Units sold</th><th>Price</th><th>Revenue</th><th>TFPR = price × TFPQ</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Firm A</td><td>1</td><td>1</td><td>1</td><td>1</td><td>1</td><td><strong>1</strong></td></tr>
<tr class="fragment"><td>Firm B, no wedge</td><td>2</td><td>4</td><td>8</td><td>½</td><td>4</td><td><strong>1</strong></td></tr>
<tr class="fragment"><td>Firm B, kept small</td><td>2</td><td>2</td><td>4</td><td>0.63</td><td>2.5</td><td><strong style="color:#ef8a6f">1.26</strong></td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<ul style="margin-top:.7em;font-size:1.15em">
<li class="fragment">Every firm sets its price the <strong>same percentage</strong> above its cost. B's cost is half, so B's price falls as it grows</li>
<li class="fragment">With no wedge, B grows until its revenue per unit of input is the same as A's: TFPQ <strong>different</strong>, TFPR <strong>the same</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">Keep B small, and it sells little at a high price: its TFPR is <strong>higher</strong>. A gap in TFPR is the sign of a wedge</li>
<li class="fragment">Khandelwal, Schott and Wei read it backwards: same percentage above cost, so a <strong>lower price</strong> means a <strong>better</strong> firm</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Grasp the Large, Let Go of the Small
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p style="font-size:.62em;margin:-.3em 0 .1em;opacity:.9">Chang-Tai Hsieh, Zheng (Michael) Song</p>
<p style="font-size:.5em;margin:0 0 .7em;opacity:.7">Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, 2015</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li class="fragment">The title is the slogan of a reform the Communist Party announced in <strong>1999</strong>: close or sell the <strong>small</strong> state firms, merge the <strong>big</strong> ones into groups the state controls</li>
<li class="fragment">The machinery of Hsieh and Klenow, on the same data, up to 2007 and 2012</li>
</ul>

---

## Motivation
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Song (2015) · 1. Motivation</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>1998: state firms have about <strong>half</strong> of industrial revenue, 60% of the capital, and <strong>4 in 10</strong> lose money</li>
<li class="fragment">Story one, <strong>"markets over Mao"</strong>: China grew because the state let its firms die or go private</li>
<li class="fragment">Story two, <strong>"state capitalism"</strong>: 67 of the 69 Chinese firms in the 2014 Fortune Global 500 belong to the state. "The state advances, the private sector retreats"</li>
<li class="fragment">Both stories are told <strong>without evidence</strong></li>
</ul>

---

## Question
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Song (2015) · 2. Question</p>
<p class="qtext">What did China do with its state firms after 1998, and how much of its output does that explain?</p>

---

## Data
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Song (2015) · 3. Data</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>The census of Chinese industrial firms, <strong>1998-2007 and 2012</strong>: the data of Hsieh and Klenow, extended</li>
<li class="fragment">The hard part: <strong>finding the state</strong>. A firm is state-owned if the state holds at least half of its capital or controls it</li>
<li class="fragment">They checked by hand the biggest 1% of firms in 2007: two thirds are controlled by the state, and almost half of these are <strong>registered as private</strong></li>
</ul>

---

## Empirical strategy
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Song (2015) · 4. Empirical strategy</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>The model of Hsieh and Klenow: for each firm, its <strong>TFPQ</strong> and its <strong>wedges</strong> on capital and labour</li>
<li class="fragment">Follow the state firms of 1998: <strong>closed</strong>, <strong>privatized</strong>, or <strong>kept</strong>? And the new ones?</li>
<li class="fragment">Then the "what if": start from 2007, give the reformed firms back their <strong>1998</strong> TFPQ and wedges, remove the new state firms. How much lower is output?</li>
<li class="fragment">Two rules of the world decide the answer: is capital available at a <strong>world</strong> interest rate? Did state firms have <strong>surplus</strong> workers?</li>
</ul>

## Results: the surprise
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Song (2015) · 5. Results</p>
<p class="qtext">Can making a state firm more productive make the economy produce less?</p>

<ul>
<li class="fragment">Yes. If the firm earns <strong>less</strong> than average on its capital and workers, a better firm pulls <strong>more</strong> of them in, to where they produce least</li>
<li class="fragment">You saw it yesterday, <strong>SOE Lab 3</strong>: "Upgrade state firms +30%" gives +15% directly, and the inputs moving to them take away about 8%</li>
<li class="fragment">So a reform of bad firms is good only if the <strong>wedges</strong> go too</li>
</ul>

## Robustness
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Song (2015) · 6. Robustness, or what a referee would ask</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li><strong>Wedge or technology?</strong> The model gives state and private firms the same technology. Brandt: "highly restrictive"; with a more flexible one, state firms' productivity grows <strong>less</strong></li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Open or closed?</strong> The sign of the result depends on it</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Where is the counterfactual?</strong> No experiment: the government chose whom to close, and the "what if" comes from the model</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>The data</strong>: no small firms, four missing years, and no value added in 2012</li>
</ul>

## Why it matters
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Hsieh and Song (2015) · 7. Why it matters</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>Neither story is right. Closing and selling state firms added <strong>little</strong>; reforming the ones the state kept added <strong>about a fifth</strong>; private firms did the <strong>rest</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">Reforming bad firms is not enough: what counts is <strong>where</strong> capital and workers go</li>
<li class="fragment">But the model cannot tell us what <strong>caused</strong> what. For that we need a wedge that was removed by someone else, on a known day</li>
</ul>

## Trade Liberalization and Embedded Institutional Reform
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p style="font-size:.62em;margin:-.3em 0 .1em;opacity:.9">Amit K. Khandelwal, Peter K. Schott, Shang-Jin Wei</p>
<p style="font-size:.5em;margin:0 0 .7em;opacity:.7">American Economic Review, 2013</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li class="fragment">Read the title: removing a <strong>trade barrier</strong> is the cause; reforming the <strong>institution</strong> built around it comes with it</li>
</ul>

---

## Motivation
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Khandelwal, Schott and Wei (2013) · 1. Motivation</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>Until <strong>1 January 2005</strong>, China's textile and clothing exports to the US, the EU and Canada had <strong>quotas</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">The quotas were set <strong>abroad</strong>. Who could use them was decided in <strong>Beijing</strong>: the government gave out export licences</li>
<li class="fragment">Officially by past exports. In practice also by <strong>politics</strong>, and selling a licence was illegal</li>
<li class="fragment">So removing the quota may remove <strong>two</strong> costs: the barrier, and the institution that managed it</li>
</ul>

---

## Question
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Khandelwal, Schott and Wei (2013) · 2. Question</p>
<p class="qtext">Did the government give the export licences to its best firms? If not, what did it cost?</p>

---

## Data
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Khandelwal, Schott and Wei (2013) · 3. Data</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>Chinese <strong>customs</strong> data, 2000-2005: for each firm, product and destination, the value and the quantity exported, so the <strong>price</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">And who owns the firm: the <strong>state</strong>, Chinese private owners, or foreigners</li>
<li class="fragment">Goods <strong>under quota</strong> until 2004 (cotton slips to the US) and similar goods <strong>never under quota</strong> (silk slips to the US)</li>
</ul>

---

## Question
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Khandelwal, Schott and Wei (2013) · 3. Data</p>
<p class="qtext">They have no TFP for most firms. How can they tell who is good?</p>

<ul>
<li class="fragment">From the <strong>price</strong>. Remember lecture 6: every firm charges the same markup, so price is always the <strong>same multiple</strong> of marginal cost</li>
<li class="fragment">So a lower price means a lower cost: a <strong>better</strong> firm, if quality is the same</li>
<li class="fragment">They also correct prices for quality: a good that sells more <strong>at the same price</strong> counts as better</li>
</ul>

## Results: what it was worth
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Khandelwal, Schott and Wei (2013) · 5. Results</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>Removing the quotas raised exporters' productivity by about <strong>7%</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">If the licences had gone to the best firms, the gain would have been smaller: the misallocated licences make it <strong>28%</strong> larger</li>
<li class="fragment">This number comes from a <strong>model</strong>, calibrated to the facts of the previous slides</li>
<li class="fragment">Textiles and clothing: <strong>15%</strong> of China's exports, <strong>13%</strong> of manufacturing jobs</li>
</ul>

## Robustness
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Khandelwal, Schott and Wei (2013) · 6. Robustness, or what a referee would ask</p>

<ul style="font-size:.52em">
<li><strong>Prices are not productivity</strong>: same markup, quality corrected from demand. The same rule of the world as Hsieh and Klenow</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>One year</strong>: in 2005 firms may have exported more to win a share of future quotas. The US and the EU did bring some back</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Subcontracting</strong>: if licence holders had good firms produce for them, the "entrants" were already producing</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>The control group</strong>: most entrants are firms adding quota goods. If they moved production from the other goods, the difference is too big</li>
</ul>

## Why it matters
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Khandelwal, Schott and Wei (2013) · 7. Why it matters</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>Trade can pay more than the models say: opening up also kills the <strong>institutions</strong> that grew around the barrier</li>
<li class="fragment">A reform from <strong>outside</strong>: a date fixed abroad did what Beijing had not done</li>
<li class="fragment">Misallocation is also about <strong>who is allowed in</strong>. Entry, exit and the firms that never appear will matter even more when we turn to <strong>growth</strong></li>
</ul>
