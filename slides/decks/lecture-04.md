## Question 2
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Almond, Li and Zhang (2019) · Land reform and the missing girls</p>
<p class="qtext">Why are first births and second births after a boy so useful? Which rival explanations do they rule out, and which not?</p>

<div class="fragment">
<ul>
<li><strong>They rule out</strong> anything that hits <strong>every</strong> family in the county that year: a harvest, a clinic, a campaign moves all the rows</li>
<li><strong>They cannot rule out</strong> something that arrives with the reform and matters <strong>only</strong> to families who want a son. Example: <strong>ultrasound</strong></li>
<li>That is why the authors look at <strong>rail links</strong> to the provincial capital, where the machines were</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Question 4
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Almond, Li and Zhang (2019) · Land reform and the missing girls</p>
<p class="qtext">State in one sentence the assumption that makes the comparison causal.</p>

<div class="fragment">
<ul>
<li>Without the reform, the gap in the share of boys between "after a girl" and "after a boy" families would have <strong>stayed the same</strong></li>
<li>We cannot see it after the reform. We can check it <strong>before</strong>: year by year, the two lines run <strong>flat and together</strong> until year 0</li>
<li>Same idea as lecture 3: <strong>parallel trends</strong>, an assumption, not a fact</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Question 5
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Almond, Li and Zhang (2019) · Land reform and the missing girls</p>
<p class="qtext">Describe the ideal experiment to learn whether higher income raises sex selection. Why could no one run it?</p>

<div class="fragment">
<ul>
<li>Take many rural families, give extra income to some <strong>chosen by lottery</strong>, and record the sex of their next children</li>
<li>Why not: decades of births, millions of families, and an experiment whose outcome is <strong>girls who are not born</strong></li>
<li>What the paper uses instead: <strong>nature's lottery</strong> (the sex of the first child) and the <strong>timing</strong> of a reform decided by others</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Question 6
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Almond, Li and Zhang (2019) · Land reform and the missing girls</p>
<p class="qtext">Explain in your words why the sex ratio of first births does not change after the reform.</p>

<div class="fragment">
<ul>
<li><strong>Nature decides</strong> the first child: about 1.05 boys per girl, before and after</li>
<li>Families select only when they <strong>already have a girl</strong> and want a son: the reason to select comes with the second birth</li>
<li>If the reform had made sons more <strong>useful</strong> on the farm, families would select already at the first birth. They do not: the <strong>income</strong> story fits better</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Question 9

<p class="qtext">The census does not ask about birth order: the authors rebuild it inside each household. Which families could be misclassified? Could unregistered girls look like "missing girls"? Which way would that push the result?</p>

<div class="fragment">
<ul>

## Question 7
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Chen, Fan, Gu and Zhou (2020) · City youths in village schools</p>
<p class="qtext">Why is 0.07 years per child both a small number and a large one?</p>

<div class="fragment">
<ul>
<li>About <strong>0.07 years</strong> of schooling per rural child, in the average county</li>
<li><strong>Small</strong>: less than a month of school per child</li>
<li><strong>Large</strong>: an average over <strong>all</strong> children; 245 million children make <strong>17.6 million</strong> extra years; every 100 youths, about <strong>21</strong> more primary and <strong>36</strong> more junior high graduates</li>

<p class="qtext">Girls gained about twice as much as boys. The authors suggest that educated young women acted as role models. How would you test this idea with data?</p>

<ul>

## A booklet that decides where you live
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:26px">
<figure style="margin:0;flex:none;text-align:center;width:408px"><img src="assets/hukou/booklet-cover.jpg" style="width:400px;border-radius:4px;display:block"><figcaption style="font-size:.4em;opacity:.7;line-height:1.25;margin-top:4px">Household Register (居民户口簿), cover of the 1996 design. Wikimedia Commons, public domain</figcaption></figure>
<ul style="font-size:.56em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:420px;flex:none">
<li>Every household has one. Each person is registered with two things:</li>
<li class="fragment">a <strong>category</strong>: agricultural or non-agricultural</li>
<li class="fragment">a <strong>place</strong>: the village, town or city where you belong</li>
<li class="fragment">Both are <strong>inherited</strong>, in practice from the mother. You could move down the ladder, hardly up</li>
<li class="fragment">Working outside your place or category: <strong>prohibited</strong></li>
</ul>
</div>

## Why: feeding the cities cheaply
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul>
<li><strong>1953</strong>: the state buys grain from farmers in fixed amounts, at <strong>low prices</strong>, to feed the cities and fund industry</li>
<li><strong>1955</strong>: grain <strong>rationing</strong> in the cities (for over 30 years); 1956, families classified as agricultural or not</li>
<li><strong>1958</strong>: the household registration regulation. To move to a city you need a <strong>job</strong> or <strong>school</strong> certificate, or a permit from the destination's <strong>police</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">After the Great Leap: urban registration is a <strong>"meal ticket"</strong>, rural registration <strong>"a license to starve"</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">1961-62: <strong>six million</strong> recent arrivals sent back to the villages. By the mid-1960s, a <strong>"caste system"</strong></li>
</ul>

<p style="font-size:.45em;opacity:.7">Naughton (2007), pp. 115-116; Cheng and Selden (1994); Tombe and Zhu (2019), p. 6</p>

---

## Changing status: the state decides
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul>
<li>Moving from rural to urban status had a name: <strong><em>nongzhuanfei</em></strong>, "from agricultural to non-agricultural"</li>
<li class="fragment">Who got it: <strong>permanent recruits</strong> of state enterprises, people whose land the state <strong>took</strong>, students admitted to <strong>university</strong>, people promoted to <strong>official posts</strong>, demobilised <strong>soldiers</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">How many: a yearly <strong>quota</strong> set in Beijing, <strong>0.15-0.2%</strong> of each place's non-agricultural population</li>
<li class="fragment">Selection by the <strong>state's needs</strong>, not by the worker's wish or by the market. Keep this in mind for next week: who is selected, and by what criterion, will change</li>
</ul>

<p style="font-size:.45em;opacity:.7">Chan and Buckingham (2008), pp. 590-591</p>

<ul>
<li>A job in a city factory paid more, and came with a ration card, a house, a pension</li>
<li class="fragment">Most people could not take it. In 1978 about <strong>four in five</strong> Chinese lived in the countryside</li>

## Two warnings
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul>
<li><strong>A wage gap is not a welfare gap.</strong> The last worker who stays is <strong>indifferent</strong>: the city pays τ more, and moving costs τ. The gap tells us about the <strong>barrier</strong>, not about who is worse off</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>τ is a cost, not a tax.</strong> Nobody collects it. Lost rations, lost land, lost schooling: no one gets them. So the output is <strong>lost</strong>, not moved to someone else</li>
<li class="fragment">The first worker held back costs little; each extra one costs more. A <strong>small</strong> barrier is cheap, a <strong>large</strong> one very expensive</li>
</ul>
