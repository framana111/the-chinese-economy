
# The Chinese Economy
### A Journey into History and Economics
<small>Lecture 2: How do we know? The Great Famine</small>

---

## Where we stopped: the Great Leap Forward, 1958-60
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **Goal: industrialise fast**
  - Everything at once, regardless of real resource constraints: "a vision, rather than a plan"
- **Organisation**: communes; bonuses and free markets abolished; decisions decentralised; "walking on two legs"
- **Two fateful decisions**: less labour and land for farming; more grain taken by the state
- **Why nobody braked**: inflated reports — the statistical system, the leaders' "eyes and ears", collapsed

---

## What happened, 1958-62
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **1958**: the Great Leap; communes and communal kitchens; labour and land pulled out of farming; harvests reported far above reality
- **Summer 1959, Lushan**: a party conference meant to correct course. Mao turns on the critics (Peng Dehuai, minister of defence); a new Anti-Rightist campaign; **grain procurement reaches new heights**
- **Harvests fall**: grain per person 292 kg (1958), 246 (1959), 214 (1960)
- **1960**: full famine, rural, worst inland (Sichuan, Anhui, Guizhou); the coastal cities stay supplied. Soviet advisers withdrawn in the summer
- **Excess deaths**: estimates from **16.5 to 45 million**
- **After the Leap**: retreat; scarce foreign exchange goes to **grain imports**

<p style="font-size:.45em;opacity:.7">Naughton (2007), pp. 69-72, 240, 379; Meng, Qian, Yared (2015), Table 1 and fn. 1</p>

---

## "Three parts nature, seven parts man"
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **Liu Shaoqi**, President of the PRC, early 1962 (Seven Thousand Cadres Conference): the famine was **30% natural disaster, 70% human error** (三分天灾，七分人祸)
- **The Party, 1981**: mainly the mistakes of the Great Leap and of the campaign against "right deviation", plus natural disasters and the split with the Soviet Union
- **Meng, Qian and Yared, 2015**: the way grain was procured accounts for about **32-43%** of famine deaths

<p class="fragment" style="font-size:.85em;color:#ffcf5a;font-weight:700;margin-top:1em">What is the difference between these numbers?</p>

---

## The puzzle
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

> In 1959-61, average food availability in the Chinese countryside
> was enough to feed the population.
>
> **So why did 16.5 to 45 million people die?**

- After the state took its share, the rural average in 1959 was about **2,300 kcal a day**; the survival benchmark is about **800**
- Production per person fell about 15% in 1959 — the largest fall of the period — but stayed far above that line

<p style="font-size:.45em;opacity:.7">Meng, Qian, Yared (2015), <em>Review of Economic Studies</em> — 19 provinces, 77% of the population</p>

## The fundamental problem of causal inference
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- Helen catches the train: **Y(1)**. Helen misses it: **Y(0)**. The effect of the train is the difference
- <span class="fragment">The film shows both lives. Real life shows **one**</span>
- <span class="fragment">You, with 5 euros tonight, and you without them: same problem</span>
- <span class="fragment">**Either Y(1) or Y(0) is observed, never both.** The missing one is the **counterfactual**</span>
- <span class="fragment">The famine: the same province, in the same year, with a lower quota. Nobody will ever see it</span>

---

## The solution: randomisation
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **Give the 5 euros by lottery**: half of you win, half do not
- <span class="fragment">**Why it works**: winning has nothing to do with you: hunger, wallet, mood, things we cannot even see. With many people, the two groups end up **alike on average** in all of them</span>
- <span class="fragment">So the losers' average stands in for the winners' missing Y(0): **the gap between the two averages is the average effect**</span>
- <span class="fragment">If instead **I choose** (the hungriest, the front row), the groups differ before any money changes hands: the gap mixes the effect with the choice</span>
- <span class="fragment">Effects differ from person to person: the average hides who gains more and who gains less</span>

---

## We cannot randomise a famine
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- Nobody ran a lottery. **History** decided which provinces had high quotas, and history is not random
- <span class="fragment">So the question becomes: **can we tie the deaths to a relevant factor that was allocated *as if* at random?**</span>
- <span class="fragment">The paper's candidate: grain **expected from the weather and the land**. Nobody chose those</span>
- <span class="fragment">Before using it: what can go wrong?</span>

## Reading the table: a difference in differences
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="font-size:.6em;line-height:1.35;max-width:900px;margin:0 auto">

<li class="fragment" data-fragment-index="4">To close it: compare high and low grain <strong>at the same level of zeal</strong></li>
</ul>
