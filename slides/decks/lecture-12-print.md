## A new kind of intelligence?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Part 1 · What is intelligence?</p>

<ul style="font-size:.54em;line-height:1.42">
<li><strong>February 1996</strong>: the world chess champion, Garry Kasparov, loses a game to IBM's Deep Blue. He writes in TIME: <em>"I could feel a new kind of intelligence across the table"</em></li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>1997</strong>: Deep Blue wins the whole match</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Twenty years later</strong>, Kasparov: <em>"It was as intelligent as your alarm clock"</em></li>
<li class="fragment">The machine had not changed. What changed was what he knew of <strong>how</strong> it won: by checking positions, millions per second</li>
<li class="fragment"><span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">Is it the result that makes intelligence, or the way it is reached?</span></li>
</ul>

## Is an anthill intelligent?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Part 1 · What is intelligence?</p>

<ul style="font-size:.54em;line-height:1.42">
<li>Argentine ants, two bridges from the nest to the food: one short, one long. Offered together, the colony <strong>ends up on the short one</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">No ant knows which bridge is shorter. Each one leaves a scent trail; the short trail is walked more often, smells stronger, and attracts more ants</li>
<li class="fragment">Now give them only the long bridge, and add the short one later. The colony <strong>keeps the long way</strong></li>
<li class="fragment"><span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">Yes, in its own world: very good at the problem it was built for, poor at a new one</span></li>
</ul>

## Skilled, but slow learners
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Part 1 · What is intelligence?</p>

<ul style="font-size:.52em;line-height:1.42">
<li><strong>Skill</strong>, spring 2026: the best models finish, half of the time, tasks that take an expert about <strong>two working days</strong></li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Learning something new</strong>: Chollet's puzzle tests, new each time, easy for people</li>
<li class="fragment" style="list-style:none;margin-left:1em">2019, the first test: beaten in <strong>December 2024</strong> (76%, 88% with 172 times more computing)<br>2025, a harder one · <strong>March 2026</strong>, the third: every human tester solves it; the best machines, <strong>below 1%</strong></li>
<li class="fragment"><span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">The answer, today: enormously skilled, still poor at learning the new. A giant anthill, far more versatile</span></li>
<li class="fragment">Why an economist cares: a machine that learns new tasks fast makes the list of jobs it can do grow fast</li>
</ul>

<p class="qkick">Part 2 · The physics, and China's chase</p>

## The chip that went back and forth
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Part 2 · The physics, and China's chase</p>

<ul style="font-size:.52em;line-height:1.42">
<li>The H20: a chip Nvidia designed to be sold to China within the rules</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>April 2025</strong>: the US requires a licence for it, "for the indefinite future". Nvidia writes off <strong>$4.5 billion</strong></li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>July-August 2025</strong>: licences come back, in exchange for <strong>15%</strong> of the revenue, paid to the US government</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>2026</strong>: Beijing reportedly steers its firms towards Chinese chips. Huawei's chips can train large models, but China cannot make enough of them</li>
<li class="fragment"><span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">The trade war of lecture 9, with a new instrument: an export licence paid with a share of the sales</span></li>
</ul>

## How close is China?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Part 2 · The physics, and China's chase</p>

<ul style="font-size:.52em;line-height:1.42">
<li><strong>March 2026</strong>: the best US model leads the best Chinese one by <strong>2.7%</strong>. Since early 2025 the top spot has changed hands several times</li>
<li class="fragment">What is left is computing power: US firms have an estimated <strong>5 to 10 times</strong> more. China can build a model like the best; it is short of chips to serve it to everyone</li>
<li class="fragment">How they caught up: they <strong>learned from the leaders</strong>, partly from their models' own answers, and <strong>invented</strong> cheaper ways to train</li>
<li class="fragment"><span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">Lectures 10-11 in real time: copy while far from the frontier, invent when close</span></li>
</ul>

## The call centre
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Part 4 · The first rung</p>

<ul style="font-size:.52em;line-height:1.42">
<li>A large software company gives an AI assistant to <strong>5,172</strong> of its customer-support agents, team by team</li>
<li class="fragment">Problems solved per hour: <strong>+15%</strong> on average. Beginners and the less skilled: about <strong>+34%</strong>. The best agents: almost nothing</li>
<li class="fragment">Why? The assistant passes on what the best agents do: the know-how of the veterans reaches the newcomer on screen</li>
<li class="fragment">Customers are less angry, fewer agents quit</li>
<li class="fragment"><span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">A knowledge spillover, delivered by a machine, to the beginner</span></li>
</ul>

## Fewer first jobs
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Part 4 · The first rung</p>

<ul style="font-size:.52em;line-height:1.42">
<li>US payroll data, since late 2022. Workers aged <strong>22-25</strong> in the jobs most exposed to AI, against their peers in other jobs: <strong>−13%</strong> (August 2025), <strong>−16%</strong> (November 2025), <strong>−19%</strong> (August 2026)</li>
<li class="fragment">Software developers aged 22-25: about <strong>20% below</strong> their late-2022 peak. Those aged 41-49: <strong>up</strong> more than 20%</li>
<li class="fragment">Mostly fewer hirings, not layoffs. No sign yet of job losses in the whole economy, and the authors call it a description, not a proof of cause</li>
<li class="fragment"><span style="background:#ffcf5a;color:#1b1b1b;font-weight:700;padding:.02em .35em;border-radius:5px">The paradox: AI helps the beginner most, and firms hire fewer beginners</span></li>
</ul>
