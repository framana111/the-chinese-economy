## Filling the chain
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:22px">
<figure style="margin:0;flex:none;text-align:center;width:500px"><img src="assets/l9/tiva-dva.svg" style="width:500px;display:block"><figcaption style="font-size:.38em;opacity:.7;line-height:1.25;margin-top:4px">Domestic value added, % of China's gross exports. OECD TiVA, 2026 edition</figcaption></figure>
<ul style="font-size:.48em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:400px;flex:none">
<li>With the WTO the Chinese share first <strong>falls</strong>: assembly of imported parts booms. Low point: <strong>2004</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">Then it <strong>rises</strong>, to 84% in 2015, and stays there. Electronics: from 56% to 72%</li>
<li class="fragment">Why? Kee and Tang (2016), customs records of all Chinese exporters, 2000-2007: the same firms <strong>replaced imported parts with Chinese ones</strong>, cheaper and in more varieties</li>
<li class="fragment">The cause: cheaper inputs and foreign suppliers moving in, after the WTO. Not wages, not a change in what China exports</li>
<li class="fragment">Careful: "Chinese" here means <strong>made in China</strong>, not made by Chinese firms</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Moving up
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>After 2007 processing trade shrinks. From <strong>2011</strong>, ordinary trade is bigger than processing</li>
<li class="fragment">Wages rise: an hour of factory work cost <strong>$0.60</strong> in 2002, <strong>$1.74</strong> in 2009. Rough numbers, in dollars. The Lewis turning point (lecture 5)</li>
<li class="fragment">And Chinese firms move to the two ends: their own brands, at home first and then abroad</li>
</ul>

<p class="fragment" style="font-size:.5em;margin-top:.8em;opacity:.9">So China kept some of the assembly, and added more of the parts. Who takes the assembly that leaves?</p>

## The renminbi
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:22px">
<figure style="margin:0;flex:none;text-align:center;width:520px"><img src="assets/l9/reer.svg" style="width:520px;display:block"><figcaption style="font-size:.4em;opacity:.7;line-height:1.25;margin-top:4px">The renminbi against a basket of currencies, corrected for prices (real effective rate, 2020 = 100). BIS. <strong>Up = a stronger renminbi</strong>: the opposite of lecture 8's chart, which showed yuan per dollar</figcaption></figure>
<ul style="font-size:.5em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:370px;flex:none">
<li>Tied to the dollar at <strong>8.28</strong> yuan per dollar until <strong>July 2005</strong>. Then the central bank lets it rise slowly (fewer yuan per dollar), and holds it again at about <strong>6.83</strong> in 2008-2010</li>
<li class="fragment">In real terms it gains about <strong>50%</strong> from 2005 to 2015</li>
<li class="fragment">From 2021 to 2025 it lost about <strong>14%</strong>: Chinese prices rise more slowly than others'</li>
<li class="fragment">IMF staff (2025): undervalued by <strong>12-21%</strong>. Beijing says this overstates it</li>
<li class="fragment">Who pays for a cheap renminbi? Chinese <strong>households</strong>: imports cost them more, while exporters sell more easily abroad</li>
</ul>
</div>

## Why it rose, why it stayed down
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;gap:28px;justify-content:center;line-height:1.3;text-align:left">
<div style="width:440px"><p style="margin:0 0 .3em;color:#ffcf5a;font-weight:700;font-size:.55em">2001-2007: up to 9.8% of GDP</p><ul style="margin:0;font-size:.5em">
<li>The WTO: an export boom, much of it assembly</li>
<li class="fragment">Saving grows faster than investment, in <strong>firms, government and households</strong>. Households save for housing, schooling, health, and to marry off their sons</li>
<li class="fragment">The renminbi, tied to the dollar, falls with it (2002-05)</li>
</ul></div>
<div class="fragment" style="width:440px"><p style="margin:0 0 .3em;color:#8ab4f8;font-weight:700;font-size:.55em">After 2009: 0.2-2.6% of GDP</p><ul style="margin:0;font-size:.5em">
<li>The saving does not go away: it goes into <strong>investment</strong>, from 42% to 47% of GDP with the 2009 stimulus</li>
<li class="fragment">The renminbi gains about <strong>50%</strong> in real terms, 2005-2015</li>
<li class="fragment">Imports get dearer (raw materials), exports cheaper (machines); Chinese tourists spend abroad</li>
</ul></div>
</div>

<p class="fragment" style="font-size:.5em;margin-top:.8em">Underneath, all along: households get a small share of income and consume <strong>40%</strong> of GDP. The IMF: "Pivoting to consumption-led growth is the overarching policy priority for China"</p>

## Who paid?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>A tariff is a tax. The American importer pays it at the border. The question: does the foreign seller <strong>cut its price</strong> to make up for it?</li>
<li class="fragment">2018-19: hardly at all. US import prices rose by about the whole tariff (Amiti, Redding and Weinstein, 2019). At the border, pass-through was "high" (Cavallo, Gopinath, Neiman and Tang, 2021)</li>
<li class="fragment">In the shops prices rose less: American retailers took part of the hit on their margins (Cavallo et al.)</li>
<li class="fragment">2025: in US shops, imported goods got dearer about <strong>twice</strong> as fast as American ones (Cavallo, Llamas and Vazquez, working paper)</li>
</ul>

## Who gains, who loses from a tariff
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;justify-content:center;gap:30px;align-items:flex-start">
<figure style="margin:0;text-align:center;width:430px"><img src="assets/l9/tariff-flat.png" style="width:430px;display:block;background:#fff;border-radius:4px;padding:4px"><figcaption style="font-size:.4em;margin-top:5px;line-height:1.3;text-align:left"><strong>Flat foreign supply</strong>. American buyers lose <strong>A+B</strong>; the government collects <strong>A</strong>. The whole tariff falls on the US: net loss <strong>B</strong></figcaption></figure>
<figure class="fragment" style="margin:0;text-align:center;width:430px"><img src="assets/l9/tariff-sloped.png" style="width:430px;display:block;background:#fff;border-radius:4px;padding:4px"><figcaption style="font-size:.4em;margin-top:5px;line-height:1.3;text-align:left"><strong>Rising foreign supply</strong>. Buyers lose <strong>A+B</strong>; the government collects <strong>A+C</strong>; foreign exporters lose <strong>C+D</strong>. The US gains if <strong>C &gt; B</strong></figcaption></figure>
</div>

<ul style="font-size:.48em;line-height:1.3;margin-top:.6em">
<li class="fragment">Who pays depends on the <strong>slope of foreign supply</strong>: the flatter it is, the more the importing country pays</li>
<li class="fragment">How much trade is lost, and how big <strong>B</strong> is, depends on the <strong>elasticity of demand</strong>: how easily buyers switch to other goods</li>
<li class="fragment">So the paper measures two elasticities. That is the whole question</li>
</ul>

## Motivation
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Fajgelbaum et al. (2020) · 1. Motivation</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>For more than half a century the US led the world in <strong>lowering</strong> tariffs. In 2018 it raised them</li>
<li class="fragment">US tariffs from <strong>2.6%</strong> to <strong>16.6%</strong> on 12,043 products, worth <strong>$303 billion</strong>: 12.7% of US imports</li>
<li class="fragment">The main target, China: <strong>$247 billion</strong> of its goods, 48.8% of what it sold to the US</li>
<li class="fragment">The partners hit back: tariffs on US exports from 7.3% to 20.4%, on <strong>$127 billion</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">"Very little is known about tariff incidence": who really pays a tariff. 2018 was a chance to find out</li>
</ul>

## Question
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Fajgelbaum et al. (2020) · 2. Question</p>
<p class="qtext">When the United States taxed foreign goods in 2018, who paid: the foreign seller or the American buyer? And who, inside America, won and lost?</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li class="fragment"><strong>Who pays</strong>: did foreign sellers cut their prices, or did buyers pay the whole tariff?</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>The bill</strong>: buyers of imports, American producers, the government. What is left in total?</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>The map</strong>: which counties gained and lost? Does that say why these tariffs were chosen?</li>

## Data
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Fajgelbaum et al. (2020) · 3. Data</p>

<ul style="font-size:.52em">
<li>US customs, every month, January 2017 to April 2019: the <strong>value</strong> and the <strong>quantity</strong> of each product (a fine category, "HS-10") from each country. A product from one country is a <strong>variety</strong>: Chinese washing machines</li>

<li class="fragment">Tariffs: the official US schedules, and the partners' official documents. For the counties: jobs by sector, and the 2016 vote</li>

<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

## Results: prices
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Fajgelbaum et al. (2020) · 5. Results</p>

<figure style="margin:0 auto;width:680px;text-align:center"><img src="assets/l9/fgkk-prices.png" style="width:680px;display:block;background:#fff;border-radius:3px"><figcaption style="font-size:.4em;opacity:.7;line-height:1.25;margin-top:4px">Price before the duty (left) and with the duty (right), hit against non-hit varieties. Figure II, p. 16</figcaption></figure>

<ul style="font-size:.5em;line-height:1.3;margin-top:.6em">
<li>Imports of hit varieties fell at once: <strong>-20%</strong> in value, <strong>-23%</strong> in quantity</li>
<li class="fragment">Price <strong>before</strong> the duty: no change. Price <strong>with</strong> the duty: up by the tariff</li>
<li class="fragment">So the American buyer paid the whole tariff: <strong>complete pass-through</strong>. The foreign seller lost sales, not price</li>
<li class="fragment">The same abroad: US exporters hit by retaliation did not cut their prices either</li>
</ul>

## Robustness
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Fajgelbaum et al. (2020) · 6. Robustness, or what a referee would ask</p>

<ul style="font-size:.5em">
<li><strong>Do prices fall later?</strong> Over two, three, four months: still no</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Only some goods?</strong> Final or intermediate goods, and eleven other product features: the same result</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>Did imports just move to other countries?</strong> Imports of the whole product fell: re-routing "does not seem to be a first-order force" in this short window</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>The renminbi?</strong> Comparing within a country and month absorbs it. And nothing links the results to China's exchange rate</li>
<li class="fragment"><strong>What it cannot see</strong>, in its own words: the long run, prices in the shops, the cost of uncertainty, wage changes in foreign countries</li>
</ul>

## Why it matters
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<p class="qkick">Fajgelbaum et al. (2020) · 7. Why it matters</p>

<ul style="font-size:.55em">
<li>A tariff is a wedge "between what the importer pays and what the exporter receives". In 2018 the <strong>American buyer</strong> stood on the paying side</li>
<li class="fragment">Big transfers, a small net: from buyers of imports to <strong>producers</strong> and to the <strong>Treasury</strong></li>
<li class="fragment">Winners and losers followed the <strong>electoral map</strong>: the politics of the China shock of lecture 8</li>
<li class="fragment">Back to the iPhone: a tariff on iPhones would also tax work done in Japan, Germany, Korea and the US. (Phones escaped the 2018-19 tariffs)</li>
</ul>
