## China, around 1800

- About 381 million people in 1820 — around **36% of the world**
- About a third of world GDP (32%, Maddison); living standards close to the world average
- Over 90% rural: very high yields per hectare, low output per worker
- A dense, commercial economy: markets, money, contracts, competition — small and household-based ("bottom-heavy")

---

## …already under strain

- **Land**: nearly all cultivable land in use by 1800 — a "high-level equilibrium trap" (Elvin 1973)
- **State**: a thin layer — revenue 2-3% of national income; one official per 32,000 people (Europe: one per 600-800)
- **Inequality**: the gentry (under 1% of people) took about 20% of national income
- "The Chinese peasant is like a man standing on tiptoe up to his nose in water" (Tawney)

## Silver and opium: what the series show

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:20px">
<ul style="font-size:.5em;line-height:1.28;margin:0;text-align:left;width:390px;flex:none">
<li>Silver left China for India in the late 1830s and the 1840s (black bars below zero)</li>
<li>Then the 1850s: a very large inflow from Britain. And in the 1860s, a new outflow</li>
<li>Opium shipped from India roughly doubled in five years: <strong>21,885 chests</strong> (1834/35) to <strong>40,200</strong> (1838/39)</li>

<li>Two lines that move together can mean nothing, and can run either way: <a href="https://tylervigen.com/spurious-correlations" target="_blank">spurious correlations</a></li>

## 1912-1937: first industrialization
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- After 1911: warlords; **1927** the Nationalists unify the country; the "Nanjing decade"
- Modern factories grow **8-9% a year** — from 2% of GDP and 0.4% of the labour force
- Two patterns: **Treaty-Port** industry (light, Chinese-owned by the 1930s) and **Manchuria** (Japanese, heavy, few spillovers)

---

## 1937-1949: war and civil war
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **1931** Manchukuo; **1937** Japan invades China proper — a decade of war
- Manchuria's industry grows 14% a year (1936-42); Shanghai never regains its 1936 output before 1949
- The Nationalist state runs the war economy; **hyperinflation**: prices in 1948 = 660 million (1936 Shanghai = 100)
- 1949: the Communist victory

## The economic problem
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

> Who gives what to whom — and who decides?

- Millions of different actors: households, farms, factories
- Each must decide how much to take and how much to give to the others
- Two ways to coordinate: through **prices**, or through **quantities** (a plan)

---

## What does a price do?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- It says how **scarce** something is, relative to everything else
- It carries information nobody holds in full — *Hayek (1945)*: knowledge is dispersed; prices coordinate separate actions
- It rewards whoever economises or finds a substitute
- *Other traditions: prices also reflect technology and the distribution of income (Ricardo, Marx, Sraffa)* <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

## China's command economy
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- Initially inspired by the **USSR model**, but with differences
- The state owns the big factories; collectives own the land
- **Commands**: production targets; resources allocated directly — prices lose their role as the main signal
- The state still *sets* prices — industry dear, agriculture cheap — to channel resources to itself; compulsory grain procurement from 1953
- The Party controls managers' careers
- Labour fixed in place: tight limits on movement, jobs for life
- **Main limitations**
  - **Weak control**: weaker than in the USSR (Naughton). Symbol: Beijing could allocate at most 600 product varieties; the USSR, 60,000 by the 1970s

---

## Measuring without market prices
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- GDP adds up value at market prices — what is "value added" without them?
- China used the **Material Product System (MPS)** until 1992: it counts material production (agriculture, industry, construction, freight transport, distribution) but not *non-material* services (education, health, finance, housing, administration)
- MPS and GDP coexisted in 1985-92; GDP only from 1993
- So we look at a different indicator, the planners' main lever: **investment**

## Leaps and retrenchments
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:26px">
<ul style="font-size:.6em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:440px;flex:none">
<li>A <strong>leap</strong>: investment grows more than 20% in a year — rapid growth <em>and</em> political mobilisation; radical, "leftist" phases</li>
<li>Then <strong>retrenchment</strong>: slower investment, consolidation, often a more moderate climate (Hundred Flowers 1956-57; readjustment 1961-63; drift 1972-76)</li>
<li>"Policy swings like a pendulum from left to right and back again" — and drives, and is driven by, economic developments</li>
</ul>

## 1958 — Hukou
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **1955**: grain rationing begins (lasts over 30 years)
- **1958**: household registration formally instituted — working outside one's hukou place or category is prohibited
- After the Leap: urban registration a "meal ticket", rural registration "a license to starve"
- 1961-62: six million recent urban arrivals sent back to the villages; by the mid-1960s, a "caste system"

---

## The Great Leap Forward, 1958-60
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **Goal: industrialise fast**
  - Everything at once, regardless of real resource constraints: "a vision, rather than a plan"
- **Organisation**: communes; bonuses and free markets abolished; decisions decentralised; "walking on two legs"
- **Two fateful decisions**: less labour and land for farming; more grain taken by the state
- **Why nobody braked**: inflated reports — the statistical system, the leaders' "eyes and ears", collapsed

## How many died, and what "uneven" means
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:22px">
<ul style="font-size:.6em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:410px;flex:none">
<li>Nobody counted. Estimates of excess deaths: <strong>16.5 million</strong> (Coale 1981), <strong>30 million</strong> (Banister 1987), <strong>45 million</strong> (Dikötter 2010)</li>
<li>In 1960 the national average death rate jumps from about 10 to about <strong>19 per 1,000</strong></li>
<li>And so does the <em>spread</em> between provinces (dashed line): the same national shock, very different local outcomes</li>
<li>That is what "uneven" means: not just more deaths, but more different deaths</li>
</ul>

</div>

## The puzzle
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

> In 1959-61, average food availability in the Chinese countryside
> was enough to feed the population.
>
> **So why did 16.5 to 45 million people die?**

- After the state took its share, the rural average in 1959 was about **2,300 kcal a day**; the survival benchmark is about **800**
- Production per person fell about 15% in 1959 — the largest fall of the period — but stayed far above that line

<p style="font-size:.45em;opacity:.7">Meng, Qian, Yared (2015), <em>Review of Economic Studies</em> — 19 provinces, 77% of the population</p>

---

## The surprise
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:22px">
<ul style="font-size:.6em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:390px;flex:none">
<li>We would expect more deaths where there was <em>less</em> food</li>
<li>Across provinces, the paper finds the opposite: <strong>more deaths where predicted grain output per head was higher</strong> — only in the famine years</li>
<li>How to read the graph: each point is a year; the line says by how much mortality differs between provinces whose output differs by 1% (a coefficient). Dashed lines: 95% interval</li>
<li>About zero in normal years; clearly positive in 1958-59</li>
</ul>

</div>

## How do we measure this with bad data?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;align-items:flex-start;justify-content:center;gap:20px">
<ul style="font-size:.56em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:360px;flex:none">
<li>Official figures for output and deaths are unreliable in these years</li>
<li><strong>Proxy 1</strong>: output <em>predicted</em> from weather, geography and rural population, not the reported one</li>
<li><strong>Proxy 2</strong>: the missing children. In the 1990 census, how large is each birth cohort, in 511 counties?</li>
<li>A small cohort born in 1959-61 = more deaths and fewer births. And the counties with better land lose the most cohorts (right)</li>
</ul>


</div>

## Is it just Great Leap zeal? And how big is it?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

<div style="display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:center;gap:26px">
<ul style="font-size:.58em;line-height:1.3;margin:0;text-align:left;width:470px;flex:none">
<li>Alternatives tested: Great Leap <strong>zealousness</strong> (sown-area cuts, steel output, communal dining, purges, late liberation), urban bias, misreported output</li>
<li>More productive provinces were, if anything, <em>less</em> zealous</li>
<li>Adding the zeal controls barely changes the result</li>
<li>Back-of-the-envelope: the procurement mechanism accounts for roughly <strong>one third to 43%</strong> of famine mortality — which leaves room for the Leap's policies and for politics</li>
</ul>

## The punchline, and what the paper does not say
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **The finding**: the famine was deadliest where there had been more food
- **The authors' explanation**: a plan that fixes quantities from past output, and cannot hear a shock, takes the most from the regions that lost the most. It accounts for a large part of the deaths, not all of them
- **Not shown**: what would have happened under another rule; that the Leap's policies did not matter; that the data are exact (they are official and approximate)
- It cannot tell over-procurement from late relief; the link with the Soviet famine of 1932-33 is suggested, not shown
- **Next lecture**: how do we move from "documents" to "causes"?

## 1961-76 — Readjustment, Third Front, Cultural Revolution
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **1961-63** readjustment: investment chopped back, about 20 million workers sent to the countryside
- **1964-66** Third Front: a new expansion into the interior, "hijacked by radicalism"
- **1966-76** Cultural Revolution: 17 million urban school-leavers sent to the countryside (*xiafang*); schools and universities closed
- **1976**: Mao dies

---

## 1976-78 — The turn
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- Growth had accelerated under the Big Push — but even more in Taiwan and Hong Kong, far ahead of the PRC by 1978
- That demonstration effect convinced Chinese leaders to reform and open up
- **December 1978**: Third Plenum — ease the terms of trade with agriculture, "give farmers a chance to catch their breath"
- Deng Xiaoping consolidates power; *gaige kaifang*, reform and opening
