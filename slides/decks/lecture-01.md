## China, around 1800

- About 381 million people in 1820 — around **36% of the world**
- About a third of world GDP (32%, Maddison); living standards close to the world average
- Over 90% rural: very high yields per hectare, low output per worker
- A dense, commercial economy: markets, money, contracts, competition — small and household-based ("bottom-heavy")

---

## …already under strain
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- Land: nearly all cultivable land in use by 1800 — "high-level equilibrium trap" (Elvin 1973)
- State: a thin layer — revenue 2-3% of national income; one official per 32,000 people (Europe: one per 600-800)
- Inequality: the gentry (under 1% of people) took about 20% of income
- "The Chinese peasant is like a man standing on tiptoe up to his nose in water" (Tawney)

## From Britain to Japan
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **1895**: Sino-Japanese War → Taiwan to Japan; the Treaty of Shimonoseki lets foreigners invest — modern industry begins
- **1901**: after the Boxer Rebellion, eight powers occupy Beijing; Boxer Protocol: indemnity, tariff revenue under foreign control
- **1911**: the Qing dynasty collapses
- Share of world GDP: **32% (1820) → 9% (1913)**

---

## 1912-1937: first industrialization
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- After 1911: warlords; **1927** the Nationalists unify the country; the "Nanjing decade"
- Modern factories grow **8-9% a year** — from 2% of GDP and 0.4% of the labour force
- Two patterns: **Treaty-Port** industry (light, Chinese-owned by the 1930s) and **Manchuria** (Japanese, heavy, few spillovers)

---

## 1937-1949: war and civil war
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **1931** Manchukuo; **1937** Japan invades China proper — a decade of war
- Manchuria's industry grows 14% a year (1936-42); Shanghai never regains its 1936 output before 1949
- The Nationalist state runs the war economy; **hyperinflation**: prices in 1948 = 660 million (1936 Shanghai = 100)
- 1949: the Communist victory

## The economic problem
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

> Who gives what to whom — and who decides?

- Millions of different actors: households, farms, factories
- Each must decide how much to take and how much to give to the others
- Two ways to coordinate: through **prices**, or through **quantities** (a plan)

---

## What does a price do?
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- It says how **scarce** something is, relative to everything else
- It carries information nobody holds in full — *Hayek (1945)*: knowledge is dispersed; prices coordinate separate actions
- It rewards whoever economises or finds a substitute
- *Other traditions: prices also reflect technology and the distribution of income (Ricardo, Marx, Sraffa)* <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

---

- Prices are still there — but set by the state, not by the market <!-- .element: class="fragment" -->

## China's command economy
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- The state owns the big factories; collectives own the land
- **Commands**: production targets; resources allocated directly — prices lose their role as the main signal
- The state still *sets* prices — industry dear, agriculture cheap — to channel resources to itself; compulsory grain procurement from 1953
- The Party controls managers' careers
- Labour fixed in place: tight limits on movement, jobs for life
- Scale: Beijing allocated at most 600 product varieties; the USSR, 60,000 by the 1970s

---

## Measuring without market prices
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- GDP adds up value at market prices — what is "value added" without them?
- China used the **Material Product System (MPS)** until 1992: it counts material production (agriculture, industry, construction, freight transport, distribution) but not *non-material* services (education, health, finance, housing, administration)
- MPS and GDP coexisted in 1985-92; GDP only from 1993
- So we look at a different indicator, the planners' main lever: **investment**

---

## The planners' lever: investment

- Gross capital formation was already **26% of GDP in 1954**; it peaked near **43% in 1958-59**, fell to about **15% in 1962**, then rose again to 30-35%
- A year in which investment grows by more than **20%** is a "leap": 1953, 1956, 1958-59, 1963-66, 1970, 1978
- Between the leaps, retrenchments: 1960-62, 1967

*Source: Naughton (2007), *The Chinese Economy*, Figs. 3.1 and 3.2 — values read from the charts, approximate*

---

## Leaps and retrenchments
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- A **leap**: investment grows more than 20% in a year — rapid growth *and* political mobilisation; radical, "leftist" phases
- Then **retrenchment**: slower investment, consolidation, often a more moderate climate (Hundred Flowers 1956-57; readjustment 1961-63; drift 1972-76)
- "Policy swings like a pendulum from left to right and back again" — and drives, and is driven by, economic developments

---

## 1958 — Hukou
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **1955**: grain rationing begins (lasts over 30 years)
- **1958**: household registration formally instituted — working outside one's hukou place or category is prohibited
- After the Leap: urban registration a "meal ticket", rural registration "a license to starve"
- 1961-62: six million recent urban arrivals sent back to the villages; by the mid-1960s, a "caste system"

---

## The Great Leap Forward, 1958-60
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- "A vision, rather than a plan": do everything at once, regardless of real resource constraints
- Communes; bonuses and free markets abolished; decisions decentralised; "walking on two legs"
- Two fateful decisions: **less labour and land for farming; more grain taken by the state**
- Inflated reports: the statistical system, the leaders' "eyes and ears", collapsed

---

## The puzzle

> In 1959-61, average food availability in the Chinese countryside
> was enough to feed the population.
>
> **So why did 16.5 to 45 million people die?**

---

## The institutional mechanism
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

Meng, Qian, Yared (2015), *Review of Economic Studies* 82(4), 1568-1611

- Excess deaths: **16.5-45 million** (an explicit range; Naughton: 25-30 million through 1961)
- Per capita grain output fell about 15% in 1959 — but stayed above subsistence
- Regions with **higher** production had **higher** mortality
- Explanation: **fixed quantity procurement** and a plan that cannot collect or respond to information after an aggregate shock

---

## How do we know? Data, proxies, claims
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- There is no county-level data on output or deaths for the famine
- Proxies: missing birth cohorts, land suitability for grain, official (inflated) production figures
- The paper **documents** facts and **argues** for an institutional story
- What it does not yet tell us: what would have happened otherwise

---

## 1961-76 — Readjustment, Third Front, Cultural Revolution
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- **1961-63** readjustment: investment chopped back, about 20 million workers sent to the countryside
- **1964-66** Third Front: a new expansion into the interior, "hijacked by radicalism"
- **1966-76** Cultural Revolution: 17 million urban school-leavers sent to the countryside (*xiafang*); schools and universities closed
- *Seed*: small rural industry — the precursors of the TVEs (lecture 6)
- **1976**: Mao dies

---

## 1976-78 — The turn
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

- Growth had accelerated under the Big Push — but even more in Taiwan and Hong Kong, far ahead of the PRC by 1978
- That demonstration effect convinced Chinese leaders to reform and open up
- **December 1978**: Third Plenum — ease the terms of trade with agriculture, "give farmers a chance to catch their breath"
- Deng Xiaoping consolidates power; *gaige kaifang*, reform and opening

---

## A question
<!-- .slide: class="dense" -->

> China grew about **10% a year** from 1978 to 2005 (official data).
>
> **Did the reforms cause it — or would China have grown fast anyway?**

- Economists: continuity — a traditional economy well suited to growth
- Historians: discontinuity — 1949 was a social revolution
- Hong Kong and Taiwan accelerated even more, without socialism

**Think about it. → Lecture 2: how do we answer a question like this?**
