Each dot is a firm. On the dashed diagonal a firm has the same capital per worker as the whole industry: without wedges every firm is on it (hollow dots). Demand: one curve for all

To sell more, a firm must cut its price (σ = 3). Every firm sits on the same curve.

Workers

Top: more workers, more output, but less and less each time; revenue rises even less, because the price falls. Bottom: what one more worker adds to revenue (MRPL). The firm hires until it equals its own cost of a worker. Hollow dot: where it would stop if it paid what everybody pays, holding its capital.

Capital

The same for capital (MRPK). Cheap credit lowers the firm's cost line, so it piles up capital until its MRPK is that low.

Reading the model

Yi = Ai Kiα Li1−α   ·   TFPQi = Ai   ·   TFPRi = PiAi
MRPLi ∝ PiYi/Li = (1+τL,i) w   ·   MRPKi ∝ PiYi/Ki = (1+τK,i) r   ·   TFPRi ∝ (1+τK,i)α(1+τL,i)1−α
  • Without wedges, every firm has the same MRPL, MRPK and TFPR. The more productive firms are simply bigger: a higher TFPQ means more output, a lower price, and the same revenue per unit of input as everybody else.
  • TFPQ differences are technology; TFPR differences are the wedges. A wedge changes how much a firm uses and sells, and so its price, not what it can make with each unit of input. Press "Reallocate": the TFPQ row does not move, the other three collapse onto one line.
  • Errors in the data look like wedges. Open "Errors in the data": the true loss stays the same, the measured one rises. Press "Reallocate" with errors on: no wedge is left, yet the economist still measures a loss.
  • Cheap credit lowers the state firms' MRPK; extra workers lower their MRPL. They hold far more capital than their productivity justifies, and the good private firms are too small.
  • Licences are a wedge on entry. Press "Introduce licences": some private firms may not sell (hollow, at the bottom; how many is under More settings, a third by default). Set it to all of them and the two groups become licence holders and firms without one: rename them. Press "Reallocate": every firm that sells has the same TFPR, yet output is still below its potential. Hsieh and Klenow measure only the firms they see. Then "Remove licences": the excluded firms enter, as in Khandelwal, Schott and Wei (2013).
  • Where productivity growth comes from (the first panel under the charts, closed until you open it). It splits the change of average TFPQ (weighted by inputs) into four parts: firms that stay get better ("Upgrade"), inputs move among them ("Reallocate"), entry ("Remove licences") and exit ("Fixed cost", under More settings). This is the dynamic Olley-Pakes decomposition of Melitz and Polanec (2015).
  • Production, one firm up close (the second panel under the charts): the firm's production function, capital against workers for every firm, the demand curve, and for each input the marginal revenue product against the firm's own cost.
  • Making a favoured firm more productive is not a free lunch. It also pulls capital and workers away from firms where they produce more. In Hsieh and Song's data, with a fixed capital stock, this drag can exceed the gain (2015, Table 8).

Model of Hsieh and Klenow (2009) with the labour and capital wedges of Hsieh and Song (2015): one industry, elasticity of substitution σ = 3, capital share α = 1/3, total capital and labour fixed. Firm productivities are drawn once. The 1998 and 2007 presets set the two state-firm wedges so that state firms' output per worker and per unit of capital, relative to private firms, match Hsieh and Song's figures: about 60% and 35% in 1998, 75% and 47% in 2007 (BPEA 2015, pp. 310 and 314). Licences: private firms are left without one in an order drawn once; the licence wedge is not removed by "Reallocate". Fixed cost: firms are dropped one at a time, the smallest first, while their share of sales is below it. Everything else is illustrative.
