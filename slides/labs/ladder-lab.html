Two ways to grow. Far from the frontier, a country grows by imitating and investing; close to it, only by innovating, and innovation needs new firms to replace old ones. When should China switch, and what do subsidies buy?

The rule. Productivity grows by imitating (η·frontier) and by building on what is there (γ·own). Imitation: η = 0.06, γ = 0.92. Innovation: η = 0.018, γ = 1. Far from the frontier imitation pays (at a = 0.3: 12% against 6%); close to it, innovation pays. The two cross at a* = 0.525.

The trap. A country that never switches stops at a = 0.6, 60% of the frontier, growing at the frontier's 2%. Switching too early slows the catch-up; switching too late wastes years near the trap. Parameters are illustrative, not estimated.

The rule. Twenty product lines. Each year, in each line, a new firm succeeds with probability xE and replaces the leader one rung higher (quality ×1.2); otherwise the incumbent climbs with probability xI. Growth = ln(1.2) × [xE + (1 − xE) xI]. Here xE = 0.12 × (1 − barriers) and xI = 0.06 × √(real R&D), with real R&D = 1 + subsidy × (1 − relabelled). Illustrative numbers: the point is the direction, not the size.
